# SehatSaathi Founder Memo
## Proof of concept, legal structure, market entry, business model, financial viability, and brutal founder verdict

Prepared: May 8, 2026  
Prepared for: Founder review  
Scope: India-first launch strategy for SehatSaathi as a report-understanding, follow-up continuity, lab intake, and retest-intelligence product

---

## 1. Executive summary

The short answer is this:

SehatSaathi can become a real business, but it is unlikely to become an attractive business if it is launched primarily as a direct-to-consumer report-upload app that depends on paid user acquisition.

The product becomes much more viable if it is positioned as a healthcare continuity layer that sits at the moment of report generation, report review, or chronic follow-up. In practice, that means labs, diagnostic networks, chronic care clinics, endocrine and nephrology follow-up practices, and hospital OPD front desks are the best entry points.

The strongest truth in your favor is that the current product direction is no longer “generic AI health advice.” It has become something much more specific: a system that turns a lab report into a calmer follow-up journey, creates continuity between visits, and helps the patient arrive better prepared to the next clinical interaction.

The strongest truth against you is that India is already crowded with large consumer health brands that can outspend you on acquisition, underprice you on tests, and bundle doctor consults, pharmacy, diagnostics, and wallets in a way a startup should not try to match directly.

So the central founder question is not “Can this be built?” It already can. The real question is “Where does this sit in the value chain so that someone pays for it without the business burning cash?”

My brutal answer:

- As a consumer acquisition business: weak to dangerous early.
- As a B2B2C continuity workflow for labs and clinics: promising.
- As a white-label continuity layer for diagnostics and outpatient follow-up: strongest near-term path.
- As a trust-sensitive doctor-replacement or diagnosis engine: wrong path.

This memo recommends that SehatSaathi should not enter the market by telling users “upload your report and we will explain your health.” That message is expensive to sell, hard to defend, and creates trust risk.

Instead, SehatSaathi should enter by telling labs and clinics:

“Your patients already receive reports. We help you turn that dead PDF into a calmer follow-up layer, better repeat retention, and cleaner continuity between visits.”

That is a much stronger point of entry.

## 2. What SehatSaathi actually is

If you describe the product incorrectly, everything after that breaks: legal positioning, GTM, CAC, trust, and pricing.

SehatSaathi is not best understood as:

- an AI doctor
- a diagnosis engine
- a fitness app
- a symptom tracker
- a wellness planner
- a lab marketplace

It is best understood as:

- a health continuity layer
- a report follow-up intelligence layer
- a lab-to-patient-to-doctor bridge
- a structured between-visits operating layer

That matters because the business model changes depending on how it is described.

If you market it as “AI diagnosis from reports,” you attract regulatory scrutiny, high user skepticism, doctor resistance, and medically risky expectations.

If you market it as “a calm continuity layer that helps patients understand what deserves follow-up, what can wait, and how to prepare for review,” you create a much more defensible product category.

The current product, based on the codebase and workflow now present, has five real assets:

1. A report extraction and normalization layer.
2. A guided follow-up intelligence layer.
3. A contextual action-plan layer.
4. A retest timing and continuity layer.
5. A lab/ops workflow that can deliver the report into the patient record directly.

That is already more interesting than a plain summary app.

## 3. Proof of concept: what has actually been proven vs what has not

### 3.1 What is already proven in product terms

At a proof-of-concept level, SehatSaathi has already demonstrated a meaningful chain:

- report ingestion
- extraction
- deterministic understanding
- calmer patient-facing follow-up interpretation
- action plan generation
- doctor handoff support
- lab-side upload workflow
- retest guidance

That means the concept is not theoretical anymore. The core user journey exists.

From a product thesis perspective, three important things are already proven:

#### A. Patients do not just need “the report”

They need:

- what matters first
- what can wait
- what to ask
- what to carry into follow-up

That is a real problem.

#### B. Labs and clinics create a dead-end moment today

Most reports become a PDF, WhatsApp image, portal login, or printout. After that, continuity is weak. The patient often receives a number, not a pathway.

That gap is commercially meaningful because diagnostics providers want:

- better repeat behavior
- higher retention
- more trust
- less confusion
- more follow-up completion

#### C. There is room between raw diagnostics and clinical consultation

You do not need to replace the doctor to create value. You only need to reduce confusion and improve continuity.

That is the most promising space in the stack.

### 3.2 What is not yet proven

You should be honest about the gaps:

#### A. You have not yet proven mass user trust at scale

A founder-led pilot can hide a lot because you can explain the product personally. Public scale cannot.

#### B. You have not yet proven that patients return without being prompted

Continuity is the product promise. If users only upload once and never return, the continuity thesis weakens.

#### C. You have not yet proven that labs will pay for this rather than seeing it as a “nice extra”

The core buyer question is not “is this good UX?” It is “does this improve retention, repeat testing, operational quality, or partner differentiation enough to justify spend?”

#### D. You have not yet proven enterprise buying motion

In Indian healthcare, product value and buying behavior are not the same thing. A product may be useful but still hard to sell if it sits outside the immediate revenue workflow.

So the current state is:

- strong product proof of concept
- partial operational proof
- incomplete commercial proof

That is normal. It just means you are at the beginning of company proof, not only product proof.

## 4. Market map: where SehatSaathi sits in India

India already has strong players in adjacent categories.

### 4.1 Diagnostic and consumer health players

Large adjacent brands include:

- Tata 1mg
- Practo
- Dr Lal PathLabs
- Metropolis Healthcare
- Thyrocare
- Redcliffe Labs
- Orange Health Labs
- Healthians

These are not all the same type of company. That distinction matters.

### 4.2 What these companies are actually optimized for

#### Tata 1mg

Tata 1mg is a broad healthcare commerce platform spanning medicines, diagnostics, and doctor consultations. Its diagnostics surface emphasizes test booking, lab comparison, home collection, and specialist access. The platform claims very large consumer reach and national order volume. That makes it powerful on convenience, distribution, and transaction aggregation, but not necessarily focused on deep continuity between reports and follow-up planning.

#### Practo

Practo is stronger in doctor discovery, appointment booking, teleconsultation, and practice-management tooling. Practo Ray already serves operational workflows for clinics and has explicit ABDM positioning. That makes Practo a relevant comparison not because it is doing the same product today, but because it already owns part of the provider workflow surface that SehatSaathi may want to enter.

#### Dr Lal PathLabs, Metropolis, Thyrocare

These are major diagnostics businesses with brand trust, scale, and distribution. Their core advantage is not fancy patient UX. It is network scale, test volume, quality process, established collection and processing infrastructure, and high trust in results. A startup should not assume it can out-distribute them. But it may help them, or smaller competitors, create a better continuity layer on top of the report.

#### Redcliffe, Orange Health, Healthians

These players sit closer to digitally enabled diagnostics and home collection behavior. They are relevant because they show where the market is moving: convenience, turnaround time, app-layer experience, and user retention. They also prove that diagnostics players are willing to invest in digital engagement if it affects booking, repeat, and trust.

### 4.3 SehatSaathi’s potential category

SehatSaathi is not strongest when compared as “another app where people upload reports.”

It is strongest when positioned as:

- continuity software for diagnostics and outpatient follow-up
- an intelligence layer between report delivery and next clinical action
- a follow-up infrastructure layer that can sit inside lab, clinic, or hospital workflow

That category is less crowded than consumer diagnostics booking.

## 6. Point of entry: where this product should enter the market

This is the most important strategic section.

### 6.1 Direct-to-consumer as first entry: not recommended

A D2C-first launch sounds attractive because it feels scalable. In practice it creates four problems:

1. CAC is likely high.
2. Trust is fragile because health products are judged harshly.
3. The patient has little habit motivation after a single report event.
4. Revenue per consumer is usually too low unless you become a transaction platform.

This is exactly where many health startups burn money.

### 6.2 Best point of entry: independent labs and diagnostics groups

The best first entry point is likely independent labs and regional diagnostics chains.

Why?

- They already sit at the moment the report is created.
- They want retention and repeat behavior.
- They can deliver SehatSaathi at near-zero consumer acquisition cost.
- They can use it as a trust and convenience differentiator.
- Your lab workspace is already directionally aligned to this motion.

The pitch becomes:

“Instead of sending a PDF and losing the patient, give them a continuity layer that improves follow-up trust and keeps your brand present after the result.”

That is much more commercial than asking end users to discover you alone.

### 6.3 Second best entry: chronic specialty clinics

The next best wedge is clinics with repeat-report behavior:

- diabetes/endocrine
- nephrology
- lipid/cardiometabolic follow-up
- thyroid follow-up
- allergy/asthma clinics where context matters

These settings naturally create:

- repeated reports
- repeated interpretation need
- repeated doctor visits
- repeated patient confusion

That is where continuity actually matters.

### 6.4 Third entry: hospital follow-up desk

Hospitals are slower to sell into, but potentially stronger long term.

This can work in:

- OPD follow-up desks
- discharge-linked chronic monitoring
- internal medicine clinics
- health check programs

The downside is slower procurement, more stakeholders, and greater integration friction.

### 6.5 Entry point ranking

| Entry point | Strategic attractiveness | Sales difficulty | Trust advantage | Recommended now |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Independent labs / regional diagnostics | High | Medium | High | Yes |
| Chronic specialty clinics | High | Medium | High | Yes |
| Hospital OPD / follow-up desk | Medium-high | High | High | Later |
| Employer wellness / insurance | Medium | Medium-high | Medium | Later |
| D2C paid acquisition | Low | High burn | Low-medium | No |

## 7. How to enter without making labs, doctors, or patients insecure

This is critical. Healthcare buyers become defensive quickly if they think software is trying to replace them, bypass them, or make them legally vulnerable.

### 7.1 Labs

Labs should not feel that you are trying to become a diagnosis engine on top of their result.

The safe pitch:

“We do not reinterpret your lab as a medical diagnosis. We turn the report into a calmer follow-up layer that helps the patient understand what to review and what to prepare.”

What labs want to hear:

- we reduce patient confusion
- we improve the digital experience
- we encourage continuity
- we support repeat engagement
- we can sit inside your workflow

What they do not want to hear:

- we tell the patient what disease they have
- we redirect their authority
- we create medico-legal exposure

### 7.2 Doctors

Doctors should not feel that SehatSaathi is becoming a parallel doctor.

The safe pitch:

“This is pre-review organization, not medical replacement. It reduces noise and prepares the patient better.”

What doctors want:

- better prepared patients
- fewer confused follow-up conversations
- cleaner historical summaries
- easier triage before consult

What they fear:

- diagnostic substitution
- patient panic
- AI advice becoming clinical advice

### 7.3 Patients

Patients should not feel:

- judged
- scared
- overloaded
- sold to

They should feel:

- guided
- calmer
- prepared
- held

That emotional layer is not cosmetic. It is core trust infrastructure.

## 8. Legal and compliance working model for India

This is not formal legal advice. It is a founder operating framework based on current official policy and regulatory context.

### 8.1 What legal bucket SehatSaathi is currently closest to

Right now SehatSaathi is best treated as:

- a digital health software layer
- a health-data processing product
- a continuity and follow-up support system

It should avoid behaving like:

- a diagnostic decision-maker
- a registered medical practitioner substitute
- a telemedicine platform making clinical decisions on its own

### 8.2 DPDP Act implications

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 matters because SehatSaathi processes sensitive health-linked personal data in digital form. That means your company will function as a data fiduciary for at least some flows, and potentially a data processor in certain white-label or partner arrangements depending on contract structure.

Practical implications:

- explicit and intelligible consent matters
- purpose limitation matters
- retention limitation matters
- deletion and export rights matter
- child data handling matters
- access control matters

What this means operationally:

1. You need a very clear patient consent surface before report analysis and data linking.
2. You should minimize unnecessary profile collection.
3. You should maintain delete and export controls visibly.
4. If working with minors, parent/guardian logic becomes important.
5. Partner contracts must clearly define who is controller-like and who is processor-like in each workflow.

### 8.3 ABDM / ABHA implications

ABDM is strategically important because it offers a national digital health architecture and a trust language that Indian institutions already understand.

But ABHA linkage also increases sensitivity. If you integrate deeper into ABDM or ABHA-linked flows, then consent, data handling, and interoperability expectations become more serious.

Good news:

- ABDM is aligned with digital health continuity
- HFR/HPR/PHR positioning can support ecosystem credibility

Risk:

- if you market sloppy AI report advice while sitting near ABHA-linked identity or records, trust damage becomes worse

Recommendation:

- use ABDM alignment as a workflow credibility layer
- do not overclaim official authority
- keep user control and consent explicit

### 8.4 Telemedicine implications

India’s telemedicine guidelines are built around the Registered Medical Practitioner. This matters because software can support telemedicine, but should not casually pretend to become the practitioner.

For SehatSaathi:

- report guidance should remain non-diagnostic
- doctor questions are good
- doctor handoff is good
- triage organization is okay
- clinical instruction or prescription logic without a doctor is risky

Therefore:

- keep patient-facing language framed as follow-up support
- never imply final diagnosis from report alone
- if teleconsult is added, ensure the doctor role remains explicit and compliant

### 8.5 Clinical establishment and partner implications

If partnering with labs and hospitals, two realities matter:

1. Compliance expectations become more enterprise-like even if no one asks for them on day one.
2. Institutional buyers care about data separation, audit trails, and access control.

That means your product roadmap should include:

- role-based access
- upload audit trails
- patient ownership boundaries
- partner-specific data views
- vendor agreements and DPAs

### 8.6 Legal operating recommendation

Short version:

- stay out of diagnosis language
- stay inside follow-up support language
- use explicit consent
- build deletion/export/retention cleanly
- prepare partner contracts carefully
- if you go deeper into ABHA and clinical workflows, get proper legal review before scale

## 9. Business model options

There are five realistic revenue models. They are not equally good.

### 9.1 Model A: D2C subscription

Users upload reports and pay monthly or annually.

Pros:

- simple story
- clean unit economics on paper
- direct relationship with user

Cons:

- hard to acquire trust
- difficult habit loop
- low willingness to pay unless repeatedly sick or chronic
- competing against free or bundled alternatives

Verdict: weak as primary model.

### 9.2 Model B: Per-report fee charged to consumer

Users pay per uploaded report for summary + plan.

Pros:

- simple
- direct monetization

Cons:

- high friction
- low repeat value
- hard to justify when consumer expects free explanation or WhatsApp help

Verdict: not attractive as the main engine.

### 9.3 Model C: B2B2C lab SaaS / continuity fee

Labs pay a monthly SaaS fee, per-branch fee, or per-report usage fee.

Pros:

- aligns with workflow
- reduces consumer acquisition cost
- easier recurring revenue
- can improve partner retention and differentiation

Cons:

- enterprise/SMB sales motion needed
- buyer education required
- must prove real workflow value

Verdict: strongest early model.

### 9.4 Model D: Clinic / hospital follow-up workflow subscription

Clinics or hospitals pay for a continuity module.

Pros:

- higher ACV potential
- stronger clinical stickiness
- better chronic-care use cases

Cons:

- slower sales
- more integration friction

Verdict: strong second model after lab wedge.

### 9.5 Model E: White-label / enterprise licensing

You provide the continuity layer under partner branding.

Pros:

- can unlock bigger institutions
- good margin if product is mature

Cons:

- longer cycles
- custom demands
- support complexity

Verdict: later-stage scaling path.

### 9.6 Best model stack

Recommended revenue stack:

1. Primary: lab and clinic subscription or usage-based SaaS
2. Secondary: enterprise white-label
3. Tertiary: carefully governed teleconsult or retest partner monetization
4. Avoid relying on D2C paid subscriptions early

## 10. Pricing strategy recommendation

### 10.1 Do not price too cheaply just because India is price-sensitive

If you price too low, two bad things happen:

- buyers think it is non-essential
- you do not have room for support, onboarding, and compliance cost

### 10.2 Suggested pricing frames

#### Independent labs / small chains

- monthly branch license: INR 7,500 to INR 20,000
- or usage model: INR 8 to INR 25 per analyzed report

#### Clinics

- per doctor or per clinic subscription: INR 4,000 to INR 15,000 per month

#### Hospitals / larger groups

- annual contract with setup + usage or location pricing

### 10.3 Best early commercial structure

For early pilots:

- no large setup fee
- pilot fee or subsidized paid pilot
- clear success metrics
- defined conversion path after pilot

You should avoid free pilots that have no decision deadline.

## 12. Variable cost per report

This is where your model becomes interesting.

### 12.1 Current-state variable cost

Because the current report interpretation path is mostly deterministic after extraction, your variable cost per report can stay relatively low if:

- OCR costs are controlled
- storage costs are reasonable
- you do not use an expensive LLM for every report

### 12.2 Suggested modeled per-report cost range

Given current stack reality, a practical modeled range is:

| Component | Low volume estimate | Mid estimate | High estimate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| OCR / extraction | INR 2 | INR 6 | INR 15 |
| Storage + transfer + infra allocation | INR 1 | INR 3 | INR 6 |
| Messaging allocation | INR 1 | INR 2 | INR 4 |
| Optional OpenAI allocation | INR 0.5 | INR 3 | INR 10 |
| Support / ops allocation | INR 2 | INR 5 | INR 10 |
| **Estimated variable cost / report** | **INR 6.5** | **INR 19** | **INR 45** |

This is not a sourced market fact. It is a modeled cost framework based on:

- current code architecture
- current cloud pricing references
- the fact that Azure Document Intelligence bills per analyzed page
- the fact that OpenAI usage can be kept optional or small

### 12.3 What kills margin

Your variable margin gets damaged if you:

- use premium LLM calls on every report unnecessarily
- store and reprocess too much media carelessly
- trigger repeat extraction often
- support too many low-quality uploads manually

### 12.4 What protects margin

- deterministic report logic
- OCR confidence gating
- background processing
- lab-side upload of cleaner documents
- B2B2C distribution instead of high-CAC D2C

## 13. Financial models

These are scenario models, not certainty claims.

### 13.1 Model 1: D2C subscription model

Assumptions:

- monthly price: INR 149
- active paying users: 1,000
- monthly revenue: INR 1,49,000
- user acquisition via paid channels
- effective CAC often likely INR 300 to INR 1,500+ depending on channel and conversion

This gets ugly fast.

If CAC is even INR 500 and conversion retention is weak, you are likely underwater unless you have unusually strong retention or cross-sell.

Conclusion:

Pure D2C is financially dangerous early.

### 13.2 Model 2: Per-report consumer fee

Assumptions:

- INR 49 per report
- 5,000 paid reports/month
- gross revenue: INR 2,45,000
- variable cost at INR 19/report: INR 95,000
- contribution before fixed cost: INR 1,50,000

Looks okay on paper, but the conversion challenge is severe. Many users will hesitate to pay for something they think should be free or bundled.

Conclusion:

Not the best early model.

### 13.3 Model 3: Lab SaaS model

Assumptions:

- 20 labs or branches
- average monthly fee: INR 12,000
- revenue: INR 2,40,000 / month

At 50 labs or branches:

- revenue: INR 6,00,000 / month

At 100 labs or branches:

- revenue: INR 12,00,000 / month

This becomes meaningful because partner distribution lowers acquisition friction per end user.

### 13.4 Model 4: Usage-based lab model

Assumptions:

- 30,000 reports/month
- partner price: INR 12/report
- revenue: INR 3,60,000
- variable cost at INR 19/report would be bad
- variable cost at optimized INR 8-12/report becomes viable

This model is workable only if you keep costs lean and automate aggressively.

### 13.5 Model 5: Hybrid subscription + usage

This is probably the strongest.

Example:

- base platform fee: INR 8,000/month per lab
- plus INR 5/report above included threshold

This creates:

- predictable base revenue
- upside with volume
- more reasonable alignment for both sides

## 16. Why D2C user acquisition is probably a trap here

You already see this correctly.

Reasons:

### A. The moment of need is episodic

People do not wake up daily wanting a lab-report continuity app.

### B. Consumer willingness to pay is low unless the value is repeatedly felt

One upload is not enough.

### C. Search and ad markets are crowded

You compete indirectly with giant players for attention.

### D. Trust costs money

Healthcare is not like a casual productivity app. Confused users churn faster and complain harder.

### E. Your most efficient distribution already exists elsewhere

The lab, clinic, or hospital is the best acquisition surface because the report moment already exists there.

So yes: if you go straight into paid D2C acquisition, it can burn money very fast.

## 26. Detailed point-of-entry playbooks

This section goes one level deeper into how SehatSaathi should actually enter each segment.

### 26.1 Independent labs

This is the most practical wedge because the lab already owns:

- patient intake moment
- report generation moment
- repeat testing behavior
- WhatsApp/SMS/email distribution surface

#### Lab buyer pain points

- Patients receive the report but do not always understand what matters.
- Labs become “commodity PDF providers.”
- Follow-up is not owned.
- Repeat retention is often price- and convenience-driven.
- Front-desk and support teams answer repetitive confusion questions.

#### SehatSaathi pitch to labs

“Give every report a continuity layer. Patients receive not only the result, but a calmer next-step surface that improves trust, helps them prepare for follow-up, and keeps your brand relevant after the report is delivered.”

#### Why labs may say yes

- better patient experience without becoming a doctor
- modern digital differentiation
- possible repeat retention improvement
- more structured patient data continuity
- future ABDM and digital-record readiness story

#### Why labs may still hesitate

- “Will this increase medico-legal risk?”
- “Will patients misunderstand it as diagnosis?”
- “Will this slow my workflow?”
- “Will this improve revenue enough to matter?”
- “Do I need another software vendor?”

#### What closes the deal

Not AI language. Not fancy visuals. Not product philosophy.

What closes the deal is:

- no extra staff burden
- no disruption to report delivery
- measurable patient engagement
- clear guardrails
- low-friction onboarding

### 26.2 Chronic specialty clinics

Chronic clinics are powerful because the report is not isolated. It belongs to a larger story:

- diabetes care
- thyroid monitoring
- lipid management
- kidney follow-up
- anemia workup or repeat CBC review

#### Clinic buyer pain points

- patients forget prior reports
- patients ask broad “is this serious?” questions without context
- follow-up conversations begin from confusion instead of continuity
- staff spends time organizing non-clinical noise

#### SehatSaathi pitch to clinics

“This is a pre-review preparation layer. It helps patients arrive with the important findings, better questions, and cleaner continuity between visits.”

#### What makes this attractive

- repeated report cycles
- repeated visit cycles
- repeated clinical interpretation need
- higher lifetime value per patient

#### What makes this harder

- clinics may prefer to keep communication tightly doctor-led
- smaller clinics may resist another tool unless it clearly saves time

### 26.3 Hospitals

Hospitals can be high-value but slower.

Good fits:

- OPD follow-up programs
- preventive health packages
- medicine/endocrine/cardiology follow-up clinics
- discharge-linked chronic care continuity

#### Why hospitals are attractive

- larger volume
- stronger institutional trust
- possibility of cross-department continuity
- bigger contract value

#### Why hospitals are difficult

- slow procurement
- many decision-makers
- compliance scrutiny
- integration demands

Recommendation:

Do not start here unless you already have a champion inside.

### 26.4 Pharmacies, employers, insurers

These are real possibilities, but not best first moves.

#### Pharmacies

Could support chronic refill continuity, but they do not own the report moment as cleanly.

#### Employers

Interesting if tied to annual health checkups, but enterprise wellness is noisy and often cost-sensitive.

#### Insurers

Possible long term for claims-adjacent continuity or preventive engagement, but much slower and more regulated.

## 27. Detailed go-to-market model

### 27.1 Recommended GTM sequence

#### Step 1: founder-led sales

At this stage, no one can sell the product better than the founder, because the product category is still being defined.

You should personally handle:

- demos
- objection handling
- pilot setup
- weekly review loops
- partner feedback

#### Step 2: paid or semi-paid pilot

Free pilots create polite interest.

Paid pilots create seriousness.

Even INR 5,000 to INR 15,000 per month matters because it reveals whether the partner sees real value.

#### Step 3: measurable outcomes

Choose a small set of metrics:

- report open rate
- follow-up plan interaction rate
- repeat upload or repeat report continuity
- doctor question usage
- repeat testing retention

Without metrics, your product stays “nice.”

#### Step 4: repeatable deployment kit

Every new partner should receive:

- a simple setup checklist
- front-desk script
- patient-facing poster
- escalation/support flow
- privacy explanation
- sample success screenshots

#### Step 5: narrow specialization if needed

If one segment responds much better than others, go narrower before going broader.

Example:

If diabetes clinics or endocrine-linked labs respond strongly, build there first. Narrowness often beats horizontal storytelling.

### 27.2 Sales motion economics

Here is the founder trap to avoid:

If it takes 20 meetings and 6 weeks to close a partner worth INR 8,000 a month, the sales model is weak unless that partner expands into multi-branch volume.

So you need to know quickly:

- how many meetings per close
- how long to close
- who signs
- what the real annual value is

#### Healthy early sales pattern

- 3 to 6 relevant meetings
- 2 to 4 weeks to pilot
- 4 to 8 weeks to paid continuation

#### Unhealthy early sales pattern

- endless enthusiasm
- no usage
- no commitment
- no budget owner

### 27.3 Founder sales scripts

#### For labs

“Today, most labs send a report and lose continuity. We help you convert that result into a calmer follow-up experience that improves patient trust and keeps the next step organized.”

#### For clinics

“Patients come back with scattered reports and confusion. We prepare the patient better before the next review, without replacing the doctor.”

#### For hospitals

“This is a continuity and follow-up layer that reduces noise between diagnostics, patient understanding, and clinician review.”

## 30. Profitability sensitivity analysis

This section answers the question: what really changes whether you make money?

### 30.1 Sensitivity factor 1: acquisition model

If acquisition is:

- partner-distributed: business can work
- paid D2C: business is much weaker

This is the single biggest lever.

### 30.2 Sensitivity factor 2: price realization

If your average partner value is:

- INR 5,000/month: hard
- INR 10,000 to INR 15,000/month: workable
- INR 20,000+/month or multi-branch equivalent: attractive

### 30.3 Sensitivity factor 3: report volume vs support load

High report volume is not enough if:

- support load is high
- extraction failures are high
- partner staff need handholding

You want clean volume, not messy volume.

### 30.4 Sensitivity factor 4: clinical restraint

Overconfident output may superficially increase engagement in the short term, but it destroys trust in the medium term.

In healthcare, responsible restraint is not just ethics. It is business durability.

### 30.5 Sensitivity factor 5: repeat use

If the product creates:

- repeated plan opens
- repeated follow-up review
- repeated report continuity

then partner ROI strengthens.

If it is only a one-time curiosity layer, pricing power weakens.

### 30.6 Simple break-even math

Using a mid-stage monthly operating cost of INR 6.2 lakh:

#### At INR 10,000 monthly average partner value

- break-even partners needed: about 62

#### At INR 15,000 monthly average partner value

- break-even partners needed: about 42

#### At INR 20,000 monthly average partner value

- break-even partners needed: about 31

That is a much more promising picture than consumer break-even math.

### 30.7 Founder take-home potential

If the business reaches:

- 100 partner locations
- average realized value of INR 12,000/month
- annualized revenue of INR 1.44 crore
- annual operating cost of roughly INR 72 lakh to INR 96 lakh

then operating surplus can be roughly:

- INR 48 lakh to INR 72 lakh before tax in a disciplined structure

That is not unicorn math. It is real-business math.

This is exactly why B2B2C is the right lens here.

## 33. What the moat could actually become

At this stage, you do not have a classic hard moat. That is normal.

Early-stage founders often lie to themselves about moat. The truth is that in the beginning, the “moat” is usually some combination of:

- execution speed
- product sharpness
- workflow fit
- customer intimacy
- trust

That is true here as well.

### 33.1 Weak moats you should not overestimate

These are not durable enough on their own:

- “we use AI”
- “we summarize reports”
- “we have a health app”
- “we have one nice UI layer”
- “we support ABHA”

Big players can imitate those quickly if they care.

### 33.2 Stronger moats you could build over time

#### A. Workflow embedment

If SehatSaathi becomes part of the daily operating workflow of:

- lab front desk
- report release
- patient follow-up prep
- doctor handoff

then it becomes harder to remove.

Workflow moat is more real than AI moat.

#### B. Continuity graph

If your product becomes the place where:

- prior reports are compared
- follow-up questions accumulate
- next-step history compounds
- patient context becomes longitudinal

then you own something deeper than a one-time summary: continuity memory.

That is valuable.

#### C. Trust moat

In Indian health-tech, trust compounds slowly but matters enormously.

If patients and partners learn that SehatSaathi is:

- calm
- medically restrained
- privacy-conscious
- consistently useful

then you can win a category that more aggressive but less careful competitors damage.

#### D. Partner distribution network

If enough labs or clinics use the product, distribution itself becomes a moat:

- lower acquisition cost
- stronger data continuity
- better case-study engine
- more proof for the next buyer

#### E. Report understanding + partner workflow + patient continuity together

Any one feature is easy to copy.

The full system is harder:

- extract
- normalize
- personalize
- hand off
- retest
- retain continuity

That integrated loop is more defensible than a standalone explainer.

### 33.3 What your moat should become in three years

The ideal future moat is not “best AI report summary.”

It is:

“the most trusted continuity layer between diagnostics and follow-up in India.”

That is a much harder thing to copy, because it includes product, trust, workflow, relationships, and historical continuity.
